Electromagnetic Radiation

Every spectroscopic method shines radiation on a sample and reads what comes back. Which part of the spectrum you choose decides what you can see: spinning nuclei, rotating molecules, vibrating bonds, jumping electrons, the arrangement of atoms in a crystal, or the nucleus itself. This module maps the spectrum, then walks through each type of radiation from the longest wavelength to the shortest: what it tells you about structure and atoms, and how it is made.

Part A

The electromagnetic spectrum

Radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are all the same thing: oscillating electric and magnetic fields traveling through space at the speed of light. They differ only in how fast they oscillate. Four linked quantities describe any of them, and if you know one you know all four.

Wavelength, \(\lambda\)

The distance from one crest to the next. Units depend on the region: meters for radio, micrometers (µm) for infrared, nanometers (nm) for visible and UV, picometers (pm) for X-rays and gamma rays.

Most used for visible and ultraviolet light, in nanometers: UV-visible spectra are plotted against wavelength. Example: chlorophyll absorbs most strongly near 430 nm (blue-violet) and 662 nm (red) and reflects the green in between, which is why leaves look green.

Frequency, \(\nu\)

How many crests pass a point each second, in hertz (1 Hz = 1 s−1). All radiation travels at the same speed in a vacuum, the speed of light \(c\), so a shorter wave must oscillate faster:

\[ c = \lambda\nu \]

Most used for radio waves and microwaves, in megahertz (MHz) and gigahertz (GHz). Example: a “400 MHz” NMR spectrometer is named for the frequency at which hydrogen nuclei resonate in its magnet, and a microwave oven runs at 2.45 GHz.

Wavenumber, \(\tilde{\nu}\)

The number of waves per centimeter. Infrared and Raman spectra use it because it is directly proportional to energy.

\[ \tilde{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\ \ (\text{cm}^{-1}) \]

Most used for infrared and Raman spectra, in cm−1 (the mid-infrared light used in IR spectroscopy fits 400 to 4000 waves in each centimeter). Example: the C=O stretch of acetone appears at about 1715 cm−1, and the broad O–H stretch of an alcohol near 3300 cm−1.

Energy of a photon, \(E\)

Radiation is absorbed and emitted in packets called photons. Each carries an energy set by its frequency through Planck’s constant, \(h\). Multiply by Avogadro's number for the energy of a mole of photons, which you can compare directly with bond energies.

\[ E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = hc\tilde{\nu} \]

Most used for X-rays and gamma rays, in electronvolts (eV, keV, MeV), and in kJ mol−1 when comparing light with bond energies. Example: cobalt-60 emits gamma rays of 1.17 and 1.33 MeV, and in X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy a carbon 1s electron has a binding energy of about 285 eV.

Radio waves

λ > 1 mν < 3 × 108 HzE < 1.2 × 10−4 kJ/molExcites nuclear spin flips

Radio waves run from about a meter to many kilometers. They carry the least energy of any radiation: a 400 MHz photon, the frequency of a common NMR spectrometer, has about \(2.7 \times 10^{-25}\) J, or \(1.6 \times 10^{-4}\) kJ/mol, roughly two million times less than it takes to break a C–C bond. (Its 75 cm wavelength is just short of the 1 m edge used on this page, but NMR frequencies are still called radio frequencies.) That is far too little to move an electron or stretch a bond. It does match one very small energy gap: the gap between the two orientations of a spinning nucleus held in a strong magnet.

What it tells us about structure

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)

Nuclei such as 1H, 13C, 19F and 31P spin and act like tiny bar magnets. In a magnetic field \(B_0\) they line up either with the field (lower energy) or against it (higher energy). A radio wave of exactly the right frequency flips them. The electrons around each nucleus shield it a little, so chemically different hydrogens flip at slightly different frequencies. An NMR spectrum gives four clues:

  • Number of signals: how many kinds of hydrogen the molecule has.
  • Chemical shift, δ (ppm): what each hydrogen is near. H on a carbon bonded to O appears near 3.5 ppm, aromatic H at 7–8 ppm, an aldehyde H near 10 ppm.
  • Integration: the ratio of hydrogens of each kind.
  • Splitting (n + 1 rule): how many hydrogens sit on the neighboring carbon.

Ethanol, CH3CH2OH: three signals. A triplet at 1.2 ppm (3 H, split by the 2 H next door), a quartet at 3.7 ppm (2 H, split by the 3 H next door and shifted by the O), and a singlet near 2.6 ppm (1 H, the OH, whose position moves with concentration).

In medicine: MRI uses the same physics on the hydrogens in water and fat to image soft tissue.

How it is made

Oscillating electric current

Radio waves are made with electric current that reverses direction very quickly. An electronic oscillator (often a quartz-crystal circuit) pushes electrons back and forth along an antenna millions of times per second. Charges that accelerate radiate: each reversal sends a ripple of electric and magnetic field away from the wire at the speed of light, at the same frequency as the current.

In an NMR spectrometer a transmitter sends a short, strong radio pulse into a coil wrapped around the sample tube. Afterwards the tipped nuclei precess like spinning tops and induce a faint radio signal in the same coil, so for a moment the sample itself is a radio transmitter. A computer Fourier-transforms that signal into the spectrum.

Natural sources: lightning, the Sun, pulsars and distant galaxies.

NMR follows Skoog et al. and Pavia et al.; the natural sources follow NASA’s tour of the spectrum. References 1, 2 and 9.

Radio bands: from submarines to satellites

Radio is split into bands, each covering ten times the frequency of the one before. The frequency decides how a radio wave travels and how big its antenna has to be (ideally about a quarter of a wavelength, which at the lowest frequencies is far too tall to build). The longest waves follow the curve of the Earth and even reach submarines under the sea. Medium and short waves can bounce off the ionosphere, a layer of ionized gas that starts about 60 km up and reaches several hundred kilometers. The shortest waves pass straight through it to satellites. Pick a band to see how it travels and what it is used for.

Can a powerful transmitter break bonds?

Concept

A radio transmitter can broadcast 50,000 W, yet standing near its antenna will not break chemical bonds or cause sunburn the way ultraviolet light can. Explain why, using the energy of one photon.

Compare with a model answer

Radiation is absorbed one photon at a time, so what decides whether a bond can break is the energy of each photon, \(E = h\nu\), not the total power. A 100 MHz photon carries \(E = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s})(1.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{s}^{-1}) = 6.6 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{J}\), or about \(4 \times 10^{-5}\) kJ/mol: nearly nine million times less than the 348 kJ/mol needed to break a C–C bond.

A more powerful transmitter sends more photons each second, not more energetic ones, so it still cannot break a single bond. At very high power, radio waves can only warm tissue, which is why safety limits for transmitters are set by heating.

AM radio at night

Concept

At night you can often hear AM stations hundreds of kilometers away that you cannot pick up during the day. What changes at night, and why does it matter for AM (medium-wave) signals?

Compare with a model answer

AM stations broadcast medium waves, about 0.5 to 1.7 MHz. By day these travel mainly as a ground wave, which follows the curve of the Earth and fades within a few hundred kilometers, because the lowest layer of the ionosphere, made by the Sun’s ultraviolet light and X-rays, absorbs the waves that head upward.

At night, with no sunlight, that lowest layer disappears. The waves now reach higher layers of the ionosphere and are bent back down to Earth far away, as a skywave. FM and TV signals (VHF and UHF) pass straight through the ionosphere, so day or night they reach only as far as the horizon.

Why NMR signals are weak

Challenge

In a 400 MHz spectrometer, the two spin states of a hydrogen-1 nucleus differ in energy by \(\Delta E = h\nu\). At 25 °C (298 K), of every million hydrogen nuclei, how many more are in the lower state than in the upper state? For a gap this small, the excess fraction is \(\dfrac{N_{\text{lower}} - N_{\text{upper}}}{N_{\text{total}}} \approx \dfrac{\Delta E}{2k_{\text{B}}T}\), where \(N\) counts the nuclei in each state, \(k_{\text{B}} = 1.381 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J K}^{-1}\) is the Boltzmann constant and \(T\) the temperature.

per million

Show solution
  1. \(\Delta E = h\nu = (6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s})(4.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{s}^{-1}) = 2.650 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{J}\).
  2. The thermal energy is \(k_{\text{B}}T = (1.381 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J K}^{-1})(298\ \text{K}) = 4.115 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J}\), about 15,500 times larger than \(\Delta E\), so the two states are almost equally filled.
  3. Excess fraction: \(\dfrac{\Delta E}{2k_{\text{B}}T} = \dfrac{2.650 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{J}}{2(4.115 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J})} = 3.22 \times 10^{-5}\).
  4. Per million nuclei: \(3.22 \times 10^{-5} \times 10^{6} = 32\).

Answer: about 32 per million

Compare with a model answer

The excess is proportional to \(\Delta E\), and \(\Delta E\) is proportional to the field, so doubling the frequency to 800 MHz (an 18.8 T magnet) doubles the excess, to about 64 per million. More excess nuclei give a stronger signal, and the peaks also spread twice as far apart in hertz, so crowded spectra become easier to read. That is why research labs buy the largest magnets they can afford.

Microwaves

λ 1 mm – 1 mν 300 MHz – 300 GHzE 10−4 – 0.12 kJ/molExcites molecular rotation, electron spin

Microwaves span 1 mm to 1 m. Their photon energies match the spacing between the rotational energy levels of small molecules in the gas phase. A molecule can absorb a microwave photon and spin faster only if it has a permanent dipole moment, so the oscillating electric field has something to grab: HCl, CO and H2O absorb; N2, O2 and CO2 do not. In a magnetic field, microwaves also flip the spin of unpaired electrons.

A microwave oven works a little differently. At 2.45 GHz, water molecules in food cannot rotate freely; they twist back and forth trying to follow the oscillating field, and the jostling between neighbors becomes heat.

What it tells us about structure

Bond lengths from rotation, and unpaired electrons

Rotational (microwave) spectroscopy measures bond lengths more precisely than almost any other method. The lines of a diatomic molecule are evenly spaced by \(2B\), and the rotational constant \(B\) depends on the moment of inertia \(I = \mu r^2\), where \(\mu\) is the reduced mass of the two atoms and \(r\) is the bond length. Measure the spacing, solve for \(r\).

Carbon monoxide. Lines 3.845 cm−1 apart give \(B = 1.9225\ \text{cm}^{-1}\). Use \(c = 2.998 \times 10^{10}\ \text{cm s}^{-1}\) so the centimeters cancel. \[ I = \frac{h}{8\pi^2 c B} = 1.456 \times 10^{-46}\ \text{kg m}^2 \] \[ \mu = \frac{m_{\text{C}}\, m_{\text{O}}}{m_{\text{C}} + m_{\text{O}}} = 1.1385 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg} \] \[ r = \sqrt{\frac{I}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.456 \times 10^{-46}\ \text{kg m}^2}{1.1385 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}}} = 1.131 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m} = 113\ \text{pm} \]

Radio astronomers identify molecules in interstellar clouds (CO, H2O, HCN, even ethanol) from their rotational lines.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), at about 9.5 GHz in a 0.34 T field, detects unpaired electrons: free radicals and metal ions such as Cu2+ and Fe3+. The splitting pattern shows which nuclei the unpaired electron sits near.

How it is made

Electrons ringing a resonant cavity

Microwaves come from electrons forced to swing past a resonant metal cavity, the way air blown across the mouth of a bottle makes it ring. The workhorse is the magnetron, in every microwave oven and many radars:

  1. A heated cathode at the center gives off electrons.
  2. A high voltage (about 4 kV) pulls them outward toward the copper anode.
  3. A permanent magnet, with its field along the axis, bends their paths into curves, so they sweep around past the openings of cavities cut into the anode.
  4. The passing electrons make each cavity ring at 2.45 GHz. The ringing bunches the electrons into rotating spokes, which keep the ringing going.
  5. An antenna in one cavity picks off the energy and a metal waveguide carries it to the food.

Other sources: klystrons, Gunn diodes and solid-state synthesizers, which tune precisely, drive spectrometers. The cosmic microwave background, the 2.7 K afterglow of the Big Bang, peaks near 1 mm.

Interactive · How a microwave oven heats food

Heating food with microwaves

The magnetron sends 2.45 GHz microwaves through a short waveguide into the metal box, where they bounce off the walls. Water molecules are polar: the oxygen end is slightly negative (δ−) and the hydrogen end slightly positive (δ+). The microwaves’ electric field reverses direction about five billion times a second, and each reversal tugs the molecules around. In liquid water they are held to their neighbors by hydrogen bonds, so they can only twist back and forth, a little behind the field, bumping into each other; that jostling is heat. Dissolved salt adds to it, because its ions are pushed back and forth too. This is not a special resonance of water: water absorbs microwaves across a wide range of frequencies, and 2.45 GHz is used because it reaches a centimeter or two into food. Common question

Is microwaved food bad for your health?

No. A microwave oven only heats food. It does not make food radioactive or change it in some special way. A 2.45 GHz photon carries about 10−5 eV: more than a hundred thousand times too little to break a chemical bond, and far too little to knock out an electron. The microwaves are gone the instant the oven switches off.

Because it cooks quickly with little water, microwaving keeps as many vitamins as other cooking methods, and often more effectively than boiling, which washes vitamin C and some B vitamins out into the water.

What deserves care:

  • Cold spots. Food heats unevenly, and bacteria can survive in the cool parts. Stir, let it stand for a minute, and heat leftovers until they reach 74 °C (165 °F) all the way through.
  • Containers. Use glass, ceramic, or plastic labeled microwave-safe. Other plastics can warp, melt or let small amounts of chemicals pass into food, especially fatty food.
  • Superheated water. Water heated in a clean, smooth cup can get hotter than 100 °C without boiling, then erupt when the cup is moved or something is added. Don’t overheat it, and let it stand before taking it out.
  • A damaged door. The door’s metal mesh and seals keep the microwaves inside. In the US an oven may leak no more than 5 mW per cm2, measured 5 cm from its surface, far below a level known to harm people. Don’t use an oven whose door is bent or doesn’t close properly.
Common question

Why should we keep metals out of the microwave?

Metals are full of free electrons. Instead of soaking up microwaves the way water does, metal reflects them, and the field drives strong currents back and forth in it.

  • Sparks. Charge piles up at sharp points and thin edges: fork tines, crumpled foil, the gold or silver trim on a plate. There the electric field becomes strong enough to tear electrons off air molecules, and a spark (an arc) jumps. Sparks can scorch the oven and set food or paper alight.
  • Burning. Very thin metal, such as foil or metallic paint, carries so much current that it heats up fast and can burn.
  • Cold food and a stressed oven. A metal container shields the food, so it barely warms, and the microwaves bounced back toward the magnetron can overheat it.

The oven’s own metal walls are safe: they are designed to reflect microwaves back into the food. The door is metal too, a mesh with holes a millimeter or two across. That is far smaller than the 12 cm wavelength, so the microwaves cannot get out, while visible light, with a wavelength of only about 0.0005 mm, passes through and lets you see inside. Some ovens come with a metal rack designed for them, and some manuals allow small, smooth pieces of foil to shield thin parts of food. One should always follow the manual to ensure safe usage of the microwave device.

Safety advice and the leakage limit follow the FDA and 21 CFR 1030.10; reheating temperatures, the USDA; vitamins, Harvard Health. References 17–20.

Why the soup heats and the bowl does not

Concept

In a microwave oven, a bowl of soup gets hot while the glass bowl and the air around it stay fairly cool, and a frozen block of ice warms very slowly. Explain all three in terms of what the molecules can do.

Compare with a model answer

Microwaves heat by dielectric heating: the oscillating electric field keeps twisting polar molecules, such as water, back and forth, and as they jostle their neighbors the motion turns into heat. Liquid water molecules are polar and free to turn, so the soup heats quickly.

In glass and most ceramics the atoms are locked into a rigid network with nothing free to turn, so the bowl warms mainly by touching the hot soup. Air is mostly N2 and O2, which are nonpolar, so the field has nothing to grip. In ice the water molecules are polar but held in a crystal by hydrogen bonds, so they can barely turn; once a little liquid forms, that part heats fast, which is why defrosting can leave hot patches next to frozen ones.

Infrared

λ 700 nm – 1 mm\(\tilde{\nu}\) mid-IR 4000 – 400 cm−1E 0.12 – 171 kJ/molExcites bond vibrations

Infrared runs from the red end of the visible (700 nm) out to 1 mm. Chemists usually describe it by wavenumber: the mid-infrared, 4000 to 400 cm−1 (2.5 to 25 µm), is where molecules vibrate. Photon energies there, about 5 to 50 kJ/mol, match the spacing between vibrational energy levels, so infrared light makes bonds stretch and bend. A vibration absorbs infrared only if it changes the molecule's dipole moment. Every object warmer than absolute zero gives off infrared; your body glows most strongly near 9 µm.

How it is made

Hot objects, lasers and flames

Heat. In a hot solid the atoms jiggle, and their vibrating charges radiate a broad, continuous spectrum called blackbody radiation. The hotter the object, the shorter the wavelength of peak output (Wien's law): \(\lambda_{\max} = \dfrac{2.898 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}\cdot\text{K}}{T}\), where \(T\) is the temperature in kelvin (K) and \(\lambda_{\max}\) is the wavelength of peak output, in meters.

IR spectrometers use electrically heated sources:

  • Globar: a silicon carbide rod at about 1500 K.
  • Nernst glower: a rod of zirconium and yttrium oxides at about 1900 K.
  • Nichrome coil: about 1100 K, simple and cheap.

Lasers. The CO2 laser (10.6 µm) uses an electric discharge to set CO2 molecules vibrating; quantum cascade lasers give tunable mid-IR; diode lasers give near-IR for fiber optics (1550 nm) and TV remotes (940 nm).

Chemical reactions. Combustion leaves the hot product molecules vibrating, and they emit infrared at their own vibrational frequencies.

Common question

How do some animals sense heat?

  • Pit vipers (rattlesnakes, copperheads) have a pit between each eye and nostril. Inside, a membrane only about 15 µm thick hangs over an air space, so the infrared from warm prey warms it quickly. Heat-sensitive nerve endings in the membrane fire, and the snake’s brain merges this fuzzy heat picture with what its eyes see. Boas and pythons have similar pits along their lips, which evolved separately.
  • Vampire bats have heat-sensing pits around the nose. Up close, they help the bat find spots where warm blood flows just under the skin.
  • Fire-chaser beetles carry infrared sensors on their bodies and fly toward forest fires from many kilometers away, to lay their eggs in freshly burnt trees.

Why can’t our eyes do this? The pigment in the retina needs a photon of at least about 1.7 eV, the energy of deep red light. Infrared near 10 µm, where warm bodies glow most, carries only about 0.12 eV per photon. And an eye at 37 °C glows in that same infrared, which would swamp any image. Heat-sensing animals use separate, insulated organs that feel warming rather than catching single photons.

Common question

What do we really see in the dark?

With a thermal camera, we see a map of how much infrared each surface gives off, which mostly tracks its temperature: brighter means warmer. The colors are not real. The camera assigns them, and the same scene can be shown in grey or in any palette.

  • Glass blocks it. Ordinary glass absorbs long-wave infrared, so a thermal camera cannot see through a window, and a person’s glasses look dark. Smoke, though, lets it through better than visible light, which is why firefighters use these cameras.
  • Shiny metal fools it. Polished metal gives off little infrared and reflects its surroundings, so a hot, shiny pan can look cool. How well a surface gives off infrared is called its emissivity.
  • Night-vision goggles and trail cameras are different. They see reflected light, often near-infrared from their own infrared lamp. An 850 nm lamp glows faintly red to us; at 940 nm it is invisible to people and to most animals.

Visible light

λ 400 – 700 nmν 4.3 – 7.5 × 1014 HzE 171 – 299 kJ/mol (1.8 – 3.1 eV)Excites valence electrons

Visible light is the narrow band from 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red) that our eyes detect. Its photon energies match the gaps between the outer (valence) electron levels of many atoms and molecules: the d orbitals of transition-metal ions, the extended π systems of dyes and pigments, and the levels of atoms heated in a flame. A substance looks the color complementary to the light it absorbs: absorb violet-blue and it looks yellow-orange; absorb green and it looks purple.

What it tells us about structure

Color, concentration and the identity of elements

UV-visible absorption spectrophotometry. The wavelength of strongest absorption (\(\lambda_{\max}\)) identifies the light-absorbing group, and Beer's law, \(A = \varepsilon b c\), turns absorbance into concentration. Here \(A\) is the absorbance, \(\varepsilon\) the molar absorptivity (how strongly the substance absorbs, in L mol−1 cm−1), \(b\) the path length of light through the sample (in cm) and \(c\) the concentration (in mol L−1; this \(c\) is not the speed of light). It is the most common quantitative method in analytical and clinical labs.

Transition-metal complexes. [Ti(H2O)6]3+ absorbs near 500 nm (green) and looks purple. The absorbed energy is the d-orbital splitting, \(\Delta_{\text{o}} = hcN_{\text{A}}/\lambda \approx 240\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}\), which ranks how strongly each ligand binds. (\(\Delta_{\text{o}}\) is the splitting in an octahedral complex; \(h\) is Planck’s constant, \(c\) the speed of light, \(N_{\text{A}}\) Avogadro’s number and \(\lambda\) = 500 nm, the wavelength absorbed.)

Atomic emission and absorption. Each element emits its own set of wavelengths. Flame tests, atomic absorption (AAS) and ICP optical emission identify metals and measure them down to parts per billion. Helium was found in the Sun's spectrum in 1868, decades before anyone found it on Earth.

How it is made

An electron drops to a lower level

Visible light is released when an electron falls from a higher energy level to a lower one; the photon carries the difference, \(E_{\text{photon}} = E_{\text{upper}} - E_{\text{lower}}\). Sources differ in how the electron is lifted in the first place:

  • Heat, continuous spectrum: incandescent bulbs (tungsten near 3000 K), candle flames (glowing soot) and the Sun (about 5800 K). Try them in the blackbody explorer above.
  • Heat, line spectrum: atoms in a flame emit their own colors: sodium 589 nm (yellow), lithium 671 nm (red), strontium (red), copper (blue-green), potassium (lilac). Fireworks use these.
  • Electric discharge in a gas: neon signs (red-orange), sodium street lamps (yellow), hydrogen lamps (pink-purple).
  • Semiconductors (LEDs): electrons fall across the band gap and the photon energy matches the gap: InGaN about 2.7 eV (blue, 460 nm), AlGaInP about 2.0 eV (red, 630 nm). White LEDs are blue LEDs coated with a yellow phosphor.
  • Chemical reactions: chemiluminescence (glow sticks, luminol) and bioluminescence (fireflies).
  • Lasers: stimulated emission, such as the red He–Ne laser at 632.8 nm.

When an electron falls from n = 3, 4, 5 or 6 back to n = 2, the atom emits one photon of visible light whose energy is exactly the gap between the two levels, \(\Delta E = E_{\text{upper}} - E_{\text{lower}}\), so its wavelength is \(\lambda = hc/\Delta E\), where \(h\) is Planck’s constant and \(c\) the speed of light. The bigger the drop, the shorter the wavelength: 3 → 2 gives red, 6 → 2 gives violet. The final drop, 2 → 1, releases an ultraviolet photon that our eyes cannot see. The motion is slowed ten to a hundred million times; a real excited atom emits within about 10 to 100 ns.

The hydrogen fingerprint

An electron in a hydrogen atom can only have the energies \(E_n = -2.179 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}/n^2\), where \(n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\) is the level, called the principal quantum number. When it drops from an upper level \(n_i\) (i for initial) to a lower level \(n_f\) (f for final), it emits one photon with exactly the difference. Drops to \(n = 2\) give the four visible Balmer lines; drops to \(n = 1\) are ultraviolet and to \(n = 3\) are infrared. Pick a transition with the buttons or tap an arrow.

What the series mean. A series is the family of lines from every drop that ends on the same lower level, named after the scientist who first measured it. The lower the level the electron lands on, the bigger the drops, so each series falls in its own part of the spectrum:

Lyman
every drop that ends on \(n = 1\): ultraviolet, from 122 nm (2 → 1) down to 91 nm. Measured by Theodore Lyman, 1906–1914.
Balmer
every drop that ends on \(n = 2\): the four visible lines at 656, 486, 434 and 410 nm, then more in the near ultraviolet down to 365 nm. Described by Johann Balmer in 1885, the first series captured by a formula.
Paschen
every drop that ends on \(n = 3\): infrared, from 1875 nm (4 → 3) down to 820 nm. Measured by Friedrich Paschen, 1908.
series limit
the dashed line in the strip: the shortest wavelength in a series, given off when a free electron (from \(n = \infty\)) drops straight to that level.

The series are the evidence that an electron’s energy comes only in fixed levels: if it could have any energy, hot hydrogen would glow with a smooth rainbow instead of sharp lines. Astronomers use the Balmer lines to find hydrogen in stars and in the glowing gas clouds where stars form. More series, Brackett (ending on n = 4) and Pfund (n = 5), lie farther out in the infrared.

Ultraviolet

λ 10 – 400 nmν 7.5 × 1014 – 3 × 1016 HzE 299 – 12,000 kJ/molExcites bonding electrons; breaks bonds

Ultraviolet light runs from 400 nm down to about 10 nm. It is divided into UVA (315–400 nm), UVB (280–315 nm), UVC (100–280 nm) and vacuum UV (below about 200 nm, absorbed by the oxygen in air, so instruments must be evacuated or purged with nitrogen). Photon energies are as large as or larger than bond energies, so UV does more than excite bonding electrons (π → π* and n → π*): it can break bonds outright. That drives sunburn, the linking of neighboring DNA bases into thymine dimers, the fading of dyes and plastics, and the chemistry of the ozone layer. Below about 124 nm (above 10 eV), UV photons are ionizing.

What it tells us about structure

Conjugation, aromatic rings and orbital energies

UV-vis spectroscopy of conjugated systems. The more C=C bonds alternate with single bonds, the smaller the gap between the highest occupied and lowest unoccupied π orbitals (HOMO and LUMO), and the longer \(\lambda_{\max}\), the wavelength of strongest absorption. The interactive below shows this. Benzene rings absorb near 254 nm, which is why HPLC detectors often read at 254 nm.

Biomolecules. Proteins absorb at 280 nm (the tryptophan and tyrosine rings); DNA and RNA absorb at 260 nm (the bases). A ratio \(A_{260}/A_{280} \approx 1.8\), the absorbance at 260 nm divided by the absorbance at 280 nm, marks clean DNA.

Photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS). A helium lamp (21.2 eV) knocks valence electrons out of a molecule; their kinetic energies give the energies of the molecular orbitals directly.

How it is made

Electric discharges through gases

UV comes from the same kind of electron drop as visible light, only bigger, so the source must excite atoms and molecules harder: an electric discharge through a gas, or a very hot object.

  • Deuterium lamp: the UV source in most UV-vis spectrophotometers, a smooth continuum from 160 to 380 nm (animated below).
  • Mercury lamp: a strong line at 253.7 nm, used to disinfect water and air. Fluorescent tubes coat the glass with a phosphor that turns this UV into visible light.
  • Xenon arc lamp: a bright continuum from 200 to 1000 nm, used in fluorimeters.
  • Excimer lasers: KrF (248 nm) and ArF (193 nm), used to pattern computer chips and reshape corneas in LASIK.
  • UV LEDs: AlGaN chips (265–365 nm) for curing inks and disinfection.
  • The Sun: the ozone layer absorbs nearly all solar UV below about 290 nm.

X-rays

λ 10 pm – 10 nmν 3 × 1016 – 3 × 1019 HzE about 0.1 – 100 keVExcites inner-shell (core) electrons

X-rays have wavelengths from about 10 nm down to 10 pm. Two facts make them central to structure determination. Their wavelengths, near 0.1 nm, match the spacing between atoms in a crystal, so crystals diffract them. And their energies match the binding energies of inner-shell (core) electrons, which are different for every element. X-rays are ionizing. They pass through soft tissue but are absorbed by heavier atoms such as the calcium in bone, which is how a medical X-ray image forms.

What it tells us about structure

Where every atom sits, and which elements are present

X-ray crystallography. X-rays scattered by parallel planes of atoms reinforce each other only at angles where the extra path is a whole number of wavelengths (Bragg's law). From the angles and intensities of thousands of diffracted spots, a computer rebuilds the electron density and places every atom: bond lengths, bond angles, the shape of an enzyme's active site. Rosalind Franklin's diffraction photograph of DNA (1952) revealed its helix. Powder diffraction (XRD) identifies crystalline phases, such as which crystal form of a drug is in a tablet.

X-ray fluorescence (XRF). Knock out a core electron and the atom emits X-rays at energies unique to the element (Moseley's law, \(\sqrt{\nu} \propto (Z - 1)\), where \(\nu\) is the frequency of the element’s K\(\alpha\) X-ray, \(Z\) its atomic number, and \(\propto\) means “is proportional to”). Handheld XRF analyzers check alloys, lead in paint, and the pigments in paintings without damaging them.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). \(E_{\text{binding}} = h\nu - E_{\text{kinetic}} - \varphi\), where \(E_{\text{binding}}\) is the energy holding the electron in its orbital, \(h\nu\) the energy of the X-ray photon, \(E_{\text{kinetic}}\) the measured kinetic energy of the ejected electron, and \(\varphi\) the spectrometer's work function. The binding energy of the ejected electron identifies the element and its oxidation state: the Fe 2p peak shifts between Fe metal, Fe2+ and Fe3+.

How it is made

Fast electrons hitting a metal target

An X-ray tube is a vacuum tube with a hot filament and a metal target (copper, molybdenum, chromium or tungsten):

  1. The heated tungsten filament gives off electrons.
  2. 20 to 50 kV accelerates them toward the target.
  3. They slam into the target. About 99% of their energy becomes heat, so the target is water-cooled or spun.
  4. Bremsstrahlung ("braking radiation"): an electron swerves and slows near a nucleus and emits an X-ray of any energy up to its full energy. This gives a continuous spectrum with a sharp short-wavelength limit.
  5. Characteristic lines: an incoming electron knocks a 1s (K-shell) electron out of a target atom; an electron from the L shell drops into the hole and emits a Kα photon (from the M shell, Kβ). These energies are unique to the target element.

Other sources: synchrotrons, where electrons moving near the speed of light are bent by magnets and give off intense, tunable X-rays; and very hot gas in space, such as the Sun's million-kelvin corona.

How PES mapped the orbitals. In the 1950s and 1960s, Kai Siegbahn built X-ray photoelectron spectrometers precise enough to measure the binding energy of every electron in an atom, work that earned him a share of the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physics, and David Turner did the same for outer electrons using ultraviolet light. Their spectra showed directly what had only been inferred from ionization energies: an atom’s electrons come in groups with distinct energies. The groups are shells (1, 2, 3, …), and shells 2 and 3 split into s and p subshells whose peaks stand in the ratio 2 : 6, the number of electrons each subshell holds. Within a shell, s electrons are held more tightly than p electrons, because they spend more of their time close to the nucleus. And as the nuclear charge grows from one element to the next, every peak moves to a higher binding energy. To a good approximation (Koopmans’ theorem), each binding energy is that orbital’s energy with its sign reversed, so a photoelectron spectrum is an experimental energy-level diagram of the atom.

Gamma rays

λ < 10 pmν > 3 × 1019 HzE usually > 100 keV (> 107 kJ/mol)Excites the nucleus

Gamma rays are the most energetic radiation: wavelengths below about 10 pm and photon energies usually above 100 keV, hundreds of thousands of times more than visible light. They come from the nucleus, not from electrons. After a radioactive decay, the new nucleus is often left in an excited state, and it drops to its ground state by emitting a gamma photon, much as an excited atom emits light. Gamma and X-ray energies overlap; the name depends on the origin (nucleus or electrons), not the wavelength. Gamma rays are strongly ionizing and very penetrating, so sources are shielded with lead or thick concrete.

What it tells us about atoms

Identifying isotopes and elements, and probing iron

Gamma-ray spectroscopy. Every radioactive isotope emits gammas at its own fixed energies, a fingerprint read with a sodium iodide or germanium detector. It identifies isotopes in soil, food and water, such as 137Cs (662 keV) released by the Chernobyl and Fukushima accidents.

Neutron activation analysis (NAA). Bathing a sample in neutrons turns some stable nuclei radioactive. Their gamma energies identify the elements, and the intensities measure them, often at parts per billion, without dissolving the sample. It is used on archaeological pottery, hair and gunshot residue.

Mössbauer spectroscopy. Iron-57 nuclei absorb a 14.4 keV gamma ray. Tiny shifts in that absorption reveal iron's oxidation state (Fe2+ or Fe3+), spin state and surroundings. The Mars rover Opportunity carried a Mössbauer spectrometer and found jarosite, an iron sulfate mineral that forms in water.

In medicine: PET scans detect pairs of 511 keV gammas to map glucose use; 99mTc (140 keV) is the most used imaging isotope; 60Co beams treat tumors and sterilize medical supplies.

How it is made

Nuclear processes

  • Radioactive decay that leaves an excited nucleus: 60Co, 137Cs, 99mTc.
  • Neutron capture in a nuclear reactor, which makes 60Co from stable 59Co and makes 24Na in neutron activation analysis.
  • Matter–antimatter annihilation: a positron meets an electron and both vanish into two 511 keV gamma rays flying in opposite directions. PET scanners detect these pairs.
  • Nuclear fusion in stars, and violent cosmic events such as gamma-ray bursts.

Glossary

Short definitions of the technical words used in this module. Throughout the module, the first mention of a term on each page links here; on a computer, point at the link to read the definition without leaving the page.

Absorbance, \(A\)
How much light a sample absorbs: \(A = \log_{10}(I_0/I)\), where \(I_0\) is the light going in and \(I\) the light coming out. It rises in step with concentration (Beer’s law). \(A_{260}/A_{280}\) is the ratio of the absorbances at 260 nm and 280 nm.
Absorbed dose, gray (Gy)
The energy that ionizing radiation deposits in matter, per kilogram: 1 gray (Gy) is 1 joule per kilogram. Sterilizing medical supplies takes about 25,000 Gy (25 kGy), while a whole-body dose of a few gray received at once can kill a person.
Absorption and emission
An atom or molecule absorbs a photon when it uses the photon’s energy to jump to a higher energy level, and emits a photon when it drops back down. Either way the photon’s energy equals the gap between the two levels, \(\Delta E = h\nu\).
Annihilation
When a positron (the antimatter partner of the electron) meets an electron, both disappear and their mass becomes two gamma-ray photons of 511 keV each, flying off in opposite directions.
Antenna
A rod, wire or coil that turns an alternating current into radio waves, or radio waves back into a current. It works best when it is about a quarter or a half of a wavelength long.
Arcing (sparks)
A spark that jumps through air when the electric field becomes strong enough to tear electrons off air molecules. In a microwave oven it happens at the sharp points and thin edges of metal objects, where charge piles up.
Aromatic ring
A flat ring of atoms, such as benzene, whose π electrons are shared around the whole ring. Aromatic rings absorb ultraviolet light near 254–280 nm, which is why proteins absorb at 280 nm and why HPLC detectors often read at 254 nm.
Atomic absorption (AAS) and ICP emission
Two ways to measure metals in a solution. In atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) the sample is sprayed into a flame or furnace, and its atoms absorb their own sharp wavelengths from a lamp. In ICP optical emission spectroscopy the sample is fed into an argon plasma at 6000–10,000 K (an inductively coupled plasma), and its atoms give off their own lines. Both measure many metals down to parts per billion.
Atomic number, \(Z\)
The number of protons in an atom’s nucleus, which decides which element it is. In a nuclear symbol it is written at the bottom left, as in \(^{60}_{27}\text{Co}\).
Attenuation, mass attenuation coefficient, \(\mu/\rho\)
The weakening of a beam as it passes through matter. For X-rays and gamma rays it is exponential, \(I = I_0 e^{-(\mu/\rho)\rho x}\), where \(I_0\) is the beam going in, \(I\) the beam left after a thickness \(x\), \(\rho\) the density of the material and \(\mu/\rho\) its mass attenuation coefficient, which depends on the material and the photon energy. It is the same law as Beer’s law for light.
Avogadro’s number, \(N_{\text{A}}\)
The number of particles in one mole: \(N_{\text{A}} = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}\). Multiplying the energy of one photon by \(N_{\text{A}}\) gives the energy of a mole of photons, \(E_{\text{mol}}\).
Background radiation
The low level of ionizing radiation around us all the time, from radon gas, rocks and soil, cosmic rays, and the potassium-40 and carbon-14 in our own bodies. In the United States it gives each person about 3 millisieverts (mSv) a year, and medical doses are often compared with it.
Balmer series
The lines hydrogen gives off when an electron drops to \(n = 2\): four visible lines (656, 486, 434 and 410 nm) and more in the near ultraviolet, down to the series limit at 365 nm.
Band gap, \(E_{\text{gap}}\)
In a semiconductor or an insulator, the energy gap between the filled band of electron energies (the valence band) and the empty band above it (the conduction band). Only photons with at least this energy are absorbed, and an LED gives off photons of about this energy: \(\lambda = hc/E_{\text{gap}}\). The band gap of zinc oxide, 3.4 eV, makes it absorb ultraviolet below about 370 nm.
Beer’s law
\(A = \varepsilon b c\): absorbance equals the molar absorptivity \(\varepsilon\) times the path length \(b\) times the concentration \(c\). It lets a UV-visible spectrophotometer measure concentration.
Beta decay
A nucleus turns a neutron into a proton and throws out an electron (a beta particle, \(^{\;\,0}_{-1}\text{e}\)) and an antineutrino. In positron (β+) decay a proton becomes a neutron and a positron (\(^{\;\,0}_{+1}\text{e}\)) and a neutrino leave instead.
Bilirubin
The yellow pigment made when the body breaks down old red blood cells. The liver normally clears it; in many newborns it builds up and turns the skin yellow (jaundice). It absorbs blue light most strongly, near 460 nm, which is why blue light treats jaundice.
Binding energy
The energy needed to pull an electron out of its orbital in an atom. In X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, \(E_{\text{binding}} = h\nu - E_{\text{kinetic}} - \varphi\).
Blackbody radiation
The glow that any object gives off because of its temperature. The hotter the object, the shorter the wavelength at which it glows most brightly: an electric stove burner glows red, while the much hotter Sun looks white.
Boltzmann constant, \(k_{\text{B}}\)
The gas constant per molecule, \(1.381 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J K}^{-1}\). The product \(k_{\text{B}}T\) is the typical thermal energy of one molecule at temperature \(T\); comparing it with an energy gap tells how many molecules sit in the upper level.
Bond energy
The energy needed to break one mole of a particular bond in the gas phase, such as 348 kJ/mol for C–C and 498 kJ/mol for O=O. A photon whose energy per mole is larger than a bond energy can break that bond, which is why ultraviolet light can drive chemical reactions.
Bond length, \(r\)
The distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms. For carbon monoxide it is 113 pm.
Brackett and Pfund series
The lines of hydrogen given off when the electron drops to the fourth energy level (Brackett) or the fifth (Pfund). Both lie in the infrared.
Bragg’s law
\(n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta\), where \(n\) is the order (a whole number), \(\lambda\) the X-ray wavelength, \(d\) the spacing between planes of atoms and \(\theta\) the angle between the beam and the planes. X-rays reflected from neighboring planes of atoms in a crystal reinforce each other only at angles where the extra path, \(2d\sin\theta\), is a whole number of wavelengths. Measuring \(\theta\) gives the spacing \(d\). Diffractometers report \(2\theta\), the angle between the incoming and diffracted beams.
Bremsstrahlung
German for “braking radiation”: X-rays given off when fast electrons are slowed down by the nuclei of the target in an X-ray tube. It makes the smooth, continuous part of the X-ray spectrum.
β-Carotene
The orange pigment of carrots, a carbon chain with eleven conjugated C=C bonds. The long π system moves its absorption out of the ultraviolet into the blue (near 450 nm), so it looks orange.
Cathode and anode
The two electrodes of a vacuum tube or lamp. Electrons leave the negative cathode, often a heated filament, and are pulled to the positive anode. In an X-ray tube the anode is the metal target that the electrons strike.
Characteristic X-rays (Kα, Kβ)
X-rays of sharp, fixed energies given off when an electron drops into a hole in an inner shell of an atom. A drop from the L shell (n = 2) to the K shell (n = 1) gives Kα; a drop from the M shell (n = 3) gives Kβ. Their energies identify the element: copper Kα is 8.05 keV (0.154 nm).
Chemical shift, \(\delta\)
Where an NMR signal appears, in parts per million (ppm) from the reference compound TMS (tetramethylsilane). Nearby electronegative atoms and π bonds move a hydrogen’s signal to higher \(\delta\).
Chemiluminescence, bioluminescence
Light given off by a chemical reaction that leaves a product in an excited state, as in glow sticks and the luminol test for blood. When the reaction happens in a living thing, such as a firefly, it is called bioluminescence.
Chlorophyll
The green pigment in plants and algae that absorbs blue and red light for photosynthesis. Its ring of alternating single and double bonds surrounds a magnesium ion; it reflects the green light it does not absorb.
Complementary color
The color we see when one color is taken out of white light. A substance that absorbs green looks purple, and one that absorbs blue looks orange: we see the colors that are left.
Compton scattering
A collision in which an X-ray or gamma-ray photon bounces off an electron, hands it part of its energy, and carries on with less energy and a longer wavelength. In a gamma-ray detector it makes the broad, lower shelf (the Compton continuum) below each photopeak.
Cones and rods
The light-sensing cells of the retina. Three kinds of cone, most sensitive near 420, 534 and 564 nm, give color vision in bright light; rods, most sensitive near 498 nm, work in dim light but see no color.
Conjugation
A chain of alternating single and double bonds, which lets the π electrons spread over many atoms. The longer the conjugated chain, the smaller the energy gap and the longer the wavelength it absorbs; long chains such as the one in β-carotene absorb visible light and look colored.
Continuous spectrum, line spectrum
A continuous spectrum contains every wavelength over a range, like the glow of a hot filament or the Sun. A line spectrum contains only certain sharp wavelengths, like the light from a gas of atoms, each set by a gap between the atom’s energy levels.
Core electrons
The electrons in an atom’s inner, filled shells, such as the 1s electrons of carbon. They are held tightly, take almost no part in bonding, and need X-rays to remove; their binding energies identify the element.
Cosmic microwave background
The faint glow left over from the hot early universe, which fills all of space. It matches a blackbody at 2.7 K, which is brightest near a wavelength of 1 mm.
Crest and trough
The highest and lowest points of a wave. The distance from one crest to the next is the wavelength.
Crystal-field splitting, \(\Delta_{\text{o}}\)
The energy gap between two sets of d orbitals of a metal ion surrounded by six ligands (an octahedral complex, hence the “o”). The complex absorbs light whose photons match this gap, which gives many transition-metal compounds their color.
CT scan (computed tomography)
An X-ray method in which a tube and detectors spin around the body, taking hundreds of views that a computer combines into cross-sectional slices. It gives a larger radiation dose than an ordinary X-ray image.
Deuterium, D
Hydrogen-2, \(^{2}\text{H}\): a hydrogen isotope whose nucleus holds one proton and one neutron. Deuterium gas, D2, gives off ultraviolet light in a deuterium lamp.
Dielectric heating
Heating by a rapidly alternating electric field that keeps twisting polar molecules, such as water, back and forth; their jostling against their neighbors turns the motion into heat. It is how a microwave oven warms food.
Diffraction
The spreading and interference of waves that pass through gaps, or scatter from objects, about the size of their wavelength. X-rays diffract from crystals because their wavelength is similar to the spacing between atoms.
Dipole
Equal and opposite charges a small distance apart. Its dipole moment, the size of the charge times the distance between the charges, measures how lopsided a molecule’s charge is. A molecular vibration absorbs infrared only if it changes the molecule’s dipole moment; a dipole antenna is a pair of rods whose ends charge up oppositely as the current swings back and forth.
Doppler shift
The change in the frequency of a wave when its source, or the object reflecting it, moves toward or away from you: higher when it approaches, lower when it moves away. Radar uses it to measure speed, and Mössbauer spectroscopy uses a moving source to fine-tune the energy of its gamma rays.
Electric discharge
An electric current through a gas. Electrons pushed along by the voltage collide with atoms and molecules and excite them, and these give off light as they drop back. Neon signs, sodium and mercury lamps and the deuterium lamp all work this way.
Electromagnetic spectrum
The whole range of electromagnetic radiation, from radio waves (the longest wavelengths and lowest photon energies) through microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet and X-rays to gamma rays (the shortest wavelengths and highest energies). All of it travels at the speed of light.
Electromagnetic wave
Electric and magnetic fields oscillating at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel, each keeping the other going, moving through space at the speed of light. Light, radio waves and X-rays are all electromagnetic waves.
Electron configuration
The list of an atom’s occupied subshells and how many electrons each holds, such as 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ for neon. Photoelectron spectra confirm it peak by peak.
Electronvolt, eV
The energy an electron gains moving through a potential difference of one volt: \(1\ \text{eV} = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}\). A convenient size for one photon: visible photons carry about 1.8 to 3.1 eV.
Elementary charge, \(e\)
The charge of one proton, or (negative) of one electron: \(e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\) C. An electron pushed through a voltage \(V\) gains an energy \(eV\).
Emissivity
How well a surface gives off infrared compared with a perfect blackbody at the same temperature, from 0 to 1. Skin, wood and paint are close to 1; polished metal is low, so it can look cooler than it is to a thermal camera.
Energy gap, \(\Delta E\)
The difference in energy between two levels of an atom, molecule or nucleus, \(\Delta E = E_{\text{upper}} - E_{\text{lower}}\). A photon is absorbed or emitted only if its energy, \(h\nu\), equals this gap. In NMR it is the gap between the two orientations of a spinning nucleus in the magnet, and it grows in proportion to \(B_0\).
Energy per mole of photons, \(E_{\text{mol}}\)
The energy of one photon times Avogadro’s number, \(E_{\text{mol}} = E N_{\text{A}}\), usually in kJ mol−1, so it can be compared directly with bond energies.
Enthalpy change, \(\Delta H^{\degree}\)
The heat taken in at constant pressure when a reaction happens as written, per mole; the ° sign means standard conditions. A negative value means heat is given out, as when methane burns (−802 kJ mol−1).
Epidermis and dermis
The two layers of the skin: the thin outer epidermis, about 0.1 mm thick, whose surface is a layer of dead cells, and the thicker dermis beneath it, about 1–2 mm, which holds blood vessels, nerves and collagen.
EPR, electron paramagnetic resonance
A technique like NMR, but for unpaired electrons: in a magnetic field of about 0.34 T, an unpaired electron absorbs microwaves near 9.5 GHz. It detects free radicals and metal ions such as Cu2+ and Fe3+.
Erythema (sunburn), action spectrum
Erythema is the reddening of the skin, as in sunburn. An action spectrum shows how strongly each wavelength causes an effect: the action spectrum for sunburn is highest below about 300 nm and falls more than a thousandfold between 300 and 400 nm.
Excimer
A molecule, such as KrF, that exists only in an excited state: once it gives off its photon, it falls apart. Excimer lasers use such molecules to make intense ultraviolet light.
False color
Colors added to an image to show something we cannot see, such as temperature in a thermal image. They are chosen by the camera or the person making the picture, not by the radiation itself.
Far-UVC
Ultraviolet light of about 200–235 nm, such as the 222 nm light of krypton chloride lamps. It kills microbes, yet the dead outer layer of the skin and the tear film of the eye absorb it before it reaches living cells, so it is being tested for disinfecting rooms while people are in them.
Fingerprint region
The part of an infrared spectrum below about 1500 cm−1, crowded with bands from the bending and stretching of the whole molecule. The pattern is unique to each compound, so matching it against a library of spectra confirms what the compound is.
Fluorescence
Light given off almost at once, within about a billionth of a second, by a molecule that has just absorbed light. Some energy is lost as heat first, so the light given off has a longer wavelength than the light absorbed (the Stokes shift). Highlighter ink, optical brighteners and the security marks on banknotes glow by fluorescence under UV.
Force constant, \(k\)
The stiffness of a bond pictured as a spring, in N m−1. Stiffer bonds (double and triple bonds) have a larger \(k\) and vibrate at higher wavenumbers: \(\tilde{\nu} = \frac{1}{2\pi c}\sqrt{k/\mu}\).
Free radical
An atom or molecule with an unpaired electron, such as the hydroxyl radical, ·OH. Most are very reactive. EPR detects them, and ionizing radiation and ultraviolet light make them.
Frequency, \(\nu\)
The number of wave crests passing a point each second, in hertz (1 Hz = 1 s−1). Frequency and wavelength are linked by \(c = \lambda\nu\).
FTIR (Fourier-transform infrared) spectrometer
An infrared spectrometer that sends all wavelengths through the sample at once and uses a moving mirror to record how they interfere; a computer then turns the signal into a spectrum with a Fourier transform. It is faster and more sensitive than measuring one wavelength at a time.
Functional group
A group of atoms, such as C=O, O–H or N–H, that gives a family of compounds its typical reactions. Each absorbs infrared at its own wavenumbers, so an IR spectrum shows which groups a molecule contains.
Gamma Knife
A machine for radiosurgery with about 200 cobalt-60 sources arranged around the head. Their narrow gamma-ray beams cross at one point, which gives a high dose to a small brain tumor or a tangle of faulty blood vessels without opening the skull.
Gamma ray, \(\gamma\)
A very high-energy photon given off by an excited nucleus. Written γ in nuclear equations.
Gamma-ray burst
A brief flash of gamma rays, lasting from a few milliseconds to minutes, from a distant galaxy: the most powerful explosions known. Short bursts come from merging neutron stars, and longer ones from the collapse of very massive stars.
Germicidal UV
Ultraviolet light of about 200–280 nm (UVC), most often the 253.7 nm line of mercury lamps, used to disinfect water, air and surfaces. DNA and RNA absorb it strongly, and the damage keeps microbes from multiplying. It also burns skin and eyes.
Globar and Nernst glower
Electrically heated rods used as infrared sources in spectrometers. A Globar is a rod of silicon carbide at about 1500 K; a Nernst glower is a rod of zirconium and yttrium oxides at about 1900 K. Both glow like blackbodies, brightest in the infrared.
Greenhouse effect
The warming of a planet because gases in its air, such as CO2, H2O and CH4, let sunlight through but absorb part of the infrared that the warm ground gives off, and send some of it back down. Only molecules whose vibrations change their dipole moment absorb infrared, which is why N2 and O2 do not take part.
Ground state, excited state
The ground state is the lowest-energy arrangement of an atom, molecule or nucleus. An excited state is any higher one. It usually lasts only a short time before the system drops back and gives off the extra energy, often as a photon. An asterisk marks an excited state, as in Na* or 60Ni*.
Ground wave, skywave, line of sight
The main ways radio waves get from place to place. A ground wave follows the curve of the Earth (long waves, and medium waves by day). A skywave bounces off the ionosphere and comes down far away (short waves, and medium waves at night). Line-of-sight waves go in straight lines, so they reach only to the horizon or up to satellites (VHF and above). The very longest waves (VLF) are also guided around the planet in the gap between the ground and the ionosphere.
Gyromagnetic ratio, \(\gamma\)
A constant for each kind of nucleus that sets how fast it precesses in a magnetic field, and so which radio frequency it absorbs: \(\nu = \gamma B_0/2\pi\). For 1H, \(\gamma/2\pi = 42.58\) MHz per tesla. Dividing by \(2\pi\) turns \(\gamma B_0\), which is in radians per second, into cycles per second (Hz).
Half-life, \(t_{1/2}\)
The time it takes for half of the nuclei in a radioactive sample to decay. After two half-lives a quarter is left, after three an eighth. Half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years: 110 minutes for fluorine-18, 6.0 hours for technetium-99m and 5.3 years for cobalt-60.
Half-thickness (half-value layer)
The thickness of a material that stops half of a beam of X-rays or gamma rays. Each further half-thickness halves the beam again, so ten of them cut it to about a thousandth. For the 140 keV gamma rays of technetium-99m it is about 4.5 cm of water or 0.26 mm of lead.
Hemoglobin
The iron-containing protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. It is bright red with oxygen bound and darker without it. The two forms absorb red and infrared light differently, which is how a pulse oximeter measures oxygen.
Hertz, Hz; megahertz, MHz
The unit of frequency: 1 Hz is one cycle per second (1 s−1). 1 kHz = 103 Hz, 1 MHz = 106 Hz and 1 GHz = 109 Hz. An NMR spectrometer’s “MHz” rating is the frequency at which 1H nuclei resonate in its magnet.
HOMO and LUMO
The highest occupied and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals of a molecule. The energy gap between them sets the longest wavelength the molecule absorbs; in conjugated molecules the gap shrinks as the chain grows longer.
HPLC (high-performance liquid chromatography)
A method that separates the compounds in a mixture by pumping it at high pressure through a column packed with fine particles, so that each compound comes out at its own time. A UV detector, often reading at 254 or 280 nm, records each one as it leaves.
Hydrogen-1, 1H
The ordinary isotope of hydrogen, whose nucleus is a single proton; it makes up 99.98% of all hydrogen. It is the nucleus NMR studies most often, because nearly every organic molecule contains it and it gives a strong signal.
Integration (NMR)
The area under an NMR signal, which is proportional to the number of hydrogens that give it. In ethanol the CH3, CH2 and OH signals have areas in the ratio 3 : 2 : 1.
Ionization energy
The energy needed to remove an electron from an atom or molecule in the gas phase; the first ionization energy removes the most loosely held one. For hydrogen it is 1312 kJ/mol (13.6 eV), the energy at the limit of the Lyman series.
Ionizing radiation
Radiation whose photons carry enough energy to knock electrons out of atoms and molecules, turning them into ions: the short-wavelength end of the ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. It can break bonds in DNA, which is why exposure is kept low.
Ionosphere
A region of the upper atmosphere, from about 60 km up to several hundred kilometers, where ultraviolet light and X-rays from the Sun knock electrons off gas atoms and molecules. Its free electrons bend medium and short radio waves back down to the ground, but let VHF and higher frequencies pass straight through.
Isotope
Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons, such as carbon-12 and carbon-13. A radioactive isotope such as cobalt-60 can be identified by the energies of the gamma rays it emits.
Joule, J
The SI unit of energy. A single photon of visible light carries only about \(4 \times 10^{-19}\) J.
Kelvin, K
The SI unit of temperature. It starts at absolute zero, so a temperature in kelvin is the Celsius temperature plus 273.15.
Kinetic energy
Energy of motion. In X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, \(E_{\text{kinetic}}\) is the measured energy of the electron that the X-ray knocks out.
Klystron and Gunn diode
Two other sources of microwaves. A klystron is a vacuum tube that bunches a beam of electrons to amplify microwaves to high power, as in radar transmitters and particle accelerators. A Gunn diode is a small semiconductor device that makes low-power microwaves, as in police speed guns and some spectrometers.
Koopmans’ theorem
The approximation that the energy needed to remove an electron from an orbital equals that orbital’s energy with the sign reversed, so a photoelectron spectrum maps the orbital energies of an atom or molecule.
Lambda max, \(\lambda_{\max}\)
The wavelength at which a substance absorbs most strongly (in UV-visible spectroscopy), or at which a hot object glows most brightly (Wien’s law).
Laser
A light source that gives a narrow beam of a single wavelength with all its waves in step. The name stands for light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation.
LED, light-emitting diode
A semiconductor device that gives off light when electrons drop across its energy gap, so its color is set by the gap: \(\lambda = hc/E_{\text{gap}}\).
Ligand
A molecule or ion that bonds to a central metal ion through a lone pair of electrons, such as H2O, NH3 or Cl− in a complex. The ligands split the metal’s d orbitals, which gives many complexes their colors.
Linear accelerator (linac)
A machine that speeds electrons along a straight tube using microwaves. In radiotherapy it fires the electrons into a metal target to make X-ray beams of several MeV.
Lyman series
The lines hydrogen gives off when an electron drops to \(n = 1\): all ultraviolet, from 122 nm down to the series limit at 91 nm.
Magnetic field strength, \(B_0\)
How strong the steady magnetic field of an NMR spectrometer or MRI scanner is, measured in tesla (T). The subscript 0 marks this main field, to tell it apart from the much weaker oscillating field of the radio pulse. Both the energy gap between the spin states and the resonance frequency grow in proportion to \(B_0\).
Magnetron
The vacuum tube that makes the microwaves in a microwave oven. Electrons circling in a magnetic field sweep past cavities cut into a copper block and set them ringing at 2.45 GHz, rather like air blown across the top of a bottle.
Mass number, \(A\)
The total number of protons and neutrons in a nucleus, written at the top left of a nuclear symbol, as in \(^{60}\text{Co}\).
Melanin
The brown-black pigment of skin, hair and eyes. It absorbs ultraviolet and visible light and turns the energy into heat, which protects the cells below. UV exposure makes the skin produce more of it: a tan.
Metastable nucleus (m)
A nucleus in an excited state that lasts unusually long before giving off its gamma ray, marked with an m, as in \(^{99\text{m}}\text{Tc}\) (half-life 6.0 h).
Molar absorptivity, \(\varepsilon\)
How strongly a substance absorbs light of a given wavelength, in L mol−1 cm−1: the absorbance of a 1 mol L−1 solution in a 1 cm cell.
Mole, mol
The amount of substance that contains Avogadro’s number of particles, \(6.022 \times 10^{23}\).
Moment of inertia, \(I\)
How hard it is to set something spinning, which depends on its mass and how far that mass sits from the axis. For a diatomic molecule \(I = \mu r^2\), where \(\mu\) is the reduced mass of the two atoms and \(r\) is the bond length.
Moseley’s law
The frequency of an element’s characteristic X-rays rises with its atomic number: for K\(\alpha\) lines, \(\sqrt{\nu} \propto (Z - 1)\), where \(\propto\) means “is proportional to”. In 1913 it let Henry Moseley order the elements by atomic number.
Mössbauer spectroscopy
A method that measures how the iron-57 nuclei in a sample absorb gamma rays of 14.4 keV. The source is moved back and forth to shift the gamma-ray energy very slightly (the Doppler effect), and the shifts at which the sample absorbs reveal iron’s oxidation state and surroundings.
MRI (magnetic resonance imaging)
Medical imaging that uses the NMR of the hydrogen nuclei in water and fat. Magnetic fields that vary across the body make the resonance frequency depend on position, so a computer can build images of soft tissue without any ionizing radiation.
Near-, mid- and far-infrared
The three parts of the infrared: the near-infrared, from about 0.75 to 2.5 µm, next to red light; the mid-infrared, from 2.5 to 25 µm (4000 to 400 cm−1), where molecules vibrate and IR spectroscopy works; and the far-infrared, beyond 25 µm, toward microwaves.
Nectar guide
A pattern on a flower, often visible only in ultraviolet, that leads insects to the nectar. Pigments such as flavonols in the center of the flower absorb UV, while the petals around them reflect it.
Neutrino, antineutrino
Uncharged, nearly massless particles that carry away part of the energy of beta decay. An electron antineutrino, \(\bar{\nu}_{\text{e}}\), comes out with a beta particle; an electron neutrino, \(\nu_{\text{e}}\), comes out with a positron.
Neutron activation analysis (NAA)
Identifying and measuring the elements in a sample by bathing it in neutrons, which make some of its nuclei radioactive, and then recording the gamma rays they give off. The gamma energies identify each isotope and the intensities give the amounts, often at parts per billion.
Neutron, n
An uncharged particle in the nucleus, written \(^{1}_{0}\text{n}\). When a nucleus captures a neutron, it becomes a heavier isotope of the same element.
Night-vision goggles (image intensifier)
A device that amplifies the faint light around, including near-infrared, thousands of times and shows it on a green screen. It sees reflected light, not heat, and often carries its own infrared lamp.
NMR, nuclear magnetic resonance
A technique that puts a sample in a strong magnet and finds the radio frequencies at which its nuclei (usually 1H or 13C) flip their spins. The pattern of signals shows how the atoms in a molecule are connected. MRI uses the same physics to image the body.
Nuclear fusion
The joining of light nuclei into a heavier one, which releases energy, often with gamma rays. It powers the Sun, where hydrogen is fused into helium.
Nuclear spin
A property of some nuclei, such as 1H and 13C, that makes each behave like a tiny bar magnet. In a magnetic field the nucleus can line up with the field or against it, two spin states of slightly different energy; NMR measures the gap between them.
Optical brightener
A colorless fluorescent dye added to laundry detergent, white paper and fabrics. It absorbs invisible ultraviolet near 350 nm and gives off blue light near 440 nm, which hides the yellow tint of old fabric, so it looks whiter, and makes white clothes glow under a black light.
Orbital
A region around a nucleus, or across a molecule, where an electron is likely to be found, with a definite energy. Each orbital holds up to two electrons. Atomic orbitals are labeled by shell and shape, such as 1s, 2p and 3d.
Oscillator
An electronic circuit that makes an alternating current at a steady frequency, often kept exact by a vibrating quartz crystal. It drives the antenna of a radio transmitter.
Oxidation state
The charge an atom would have if all of its bonds were ionic, such as +3 for iron in Fe2O3. It slightly shifts binding energies and absorption, so XPS and Mössbauer spectroscopy can measure it.
Ozone layer (Chapman cycle)
The layer of the stratosphere, about 15–35 km up, where ozone (O3) is most concentrated. In the Chapman cycle, short ultraviolet splits O2, the O atoms join O2 to make O3, and O3 absorbs longer ultraviolet. Together they absorb nearly all sunlight shorter than about 290 nm.
Pair production
The change of a gamma-ray photon with more than 1.022 MeV (twice the rest energy of an electron) into an electron and a positron, next to a nucleus. It is the reverse of annihilation, and it is how telescopes such as Fermi detect high-energy gamma rays.
Paschen series
The lines hydrogen gives off when an electron drops to \(n = 3\): all infrared, from 1875 nm down to the series limit at 820 nm.
Penetration depth
How far radiation travels into a material before about two-thirds of it (all but 1/e, or 37%) has been absorbed. In skin it is a few millimeters for near-infrared but only a tiny fraction of a millimeter for far-infrared.
PET (positron emission tomography)
Medical imaging with a tracer that gives off positrons, most often FDG, a look-alike of glucose that carries a fluorine-18 atom. Each positron annihilates with an electron into two 511 keV gamma rays flying apart, and a ring of detectors maps where they came from. Tissues that use a lot of glucose, such as tumors, light up.
Phosphor
A material that absorbs light of one color and gives off another, usually longer, wavelength. White LEDs use a yellow phosphor over a blue LED.
Photobiomodulation (low-level light therapy)
Treatment with weak red or near-infrared light, from LEDs or lasers, intended to ease pain and help tissue heal. Some trials show modest benefit and others none, so the evidence is mixed.
Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES)
Measuring the kinetic energies of electrons knocked out of atoms by photons of one known energy, to find how tightly each was bound: \(E_{\text{binding}} = h\nu - E_{\text{kinetic}}\). With X-rays it is called XPS; with ultraviolet light, UPS.
Photokeratitis (“arc eye”)
A painful sunburn of the cornea, the clear front surface of the eye, caused by UVB or UVC from welding arcs, germicidal lamps or sunlight reflected off snow (snow blindness). It usually heals in a day or two.
Photolithography, photoresist
The process that prints circuit patterns onto computer chips. A silicon wafer is coated with a light-sensitive polymer, the photoresist; ultraviolet light shines through a patterned mask onto it, the exposed (or unexposed) resist is washed away, and the pattern is etched into the chip. Shorter wavelengths print smaller features.
Photon
A single packet (quantum) of electromagnetic radiation. Its energy depends only on its frequency, \(E = h\nu\), so in equations a photon is often written simply as \(h\nu\).
Photopeak
The sharp peak in a gamma-ray spectrum made when the detector absorbs a gamma ray’s whole energy. Its position gives the gamma ray’s energy, which identifies the isotope.
Phototherapy
Treatment with light. Blue light, near 460 nm, treats newborn jaundice: bilirubin in the skin absorbs it and changes into forms the baby can get rid of without the liver.
π → π* and n → π* transitions
The electron jumps behind most ultraviolet and visible absorption by organic molecules. In a π → π* transition an electron moves from a bonding π orbital to an antibonding π* orbital; in an n → π* transition it moves from a nonbonding lone pair, such as one on the O of C=O, to π*. Conjugation lowers the energy of both.
Pit organ
A heat-sensing organ of pit vipers, boas and pythons: a thin membrane over an air space, warmed by the infrared from nearby warm animals and wired to heat-sensitive nerve endings.
Planck’s constant, \(h\)
The constant that links the energy of a photon to its frequency, \(E = h\nu\): \(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}\).
Polar molecule
A molecule with a permanent dipole: one end slightly negative (δ−) and the other slightly positive (δ+), as in H2O and HCl. Polar molecules absorb microwaves; nonpolar ones such as N2, O2 and CO2 do not.
Polyene
A molecule with a chain of several C=C double bonds, usually alternating with single bonds (conjugated), such as 1,3-butadiene or β-carotene.
Positron
The antimatter partner of the electron: the same mass, but a positive charge. Written \(^{\;\,0}_{+1}\text{e}\) or e+.
Precess, precession
To precess is to wobble so that the axis of a spinning object sweeps out a cone. A tilted spinning top does this: instead of falling over, its axis slowly circles around the vertical. A nucleus spinning in a magnetic field precesses around the field direction in the same way, at exactly the frequency it absorbs, \(\nu = \gamma B_0/2\pi\) (400 MHz for 1H in a 9.40 T magnet). After a radio pulse tips the nuclei, their precessing magnetism induces the faint radio signal that an NMR spectrometer records.
Principal quantum number, \(n\)
The whole number (1, 2, 3, …) that labels the energy levels of an electron in an atom. In hydrogen, \(E_n = -2.179 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}/n^2\). A jump from an upper level \(n_i\) to a lower level \(n_f\) gives off one photon.
Pulsar
A rapidly spinning neutron star, the collapsed core of an exploded star, that sweeps beams of radio waves (and sometimes X-rays and gamma rays) across space like a lighthouse, so that we see regular pulses.
Pulse oximeter
A clip-on sensor that shines red (660 nm) and infrared (940 nm) light through a finger. Hemoglobin with and without oxygen absorbs these differently, so the ratio of the signals gives the blood’s oxygen level.
Quantized
Allowed to have only certain separate values. The energies of electrons in atoms, of molecular vibrations and rotations, and of nuclear spins in a magnet are all quantized, which is why each absorbs or emits only photons of particular energies.
Quantum cascade laser
A semiconductor laser built from many thin layers, in which each electron steps down a staircase of energy levels and gives off an infrared photon at each step. The thickness of the layers sets the wavelength, so it can be designed for the mid-infrared, for example to detect particular gases.
Radar
Short for radio detection and ranging. A dish sends out short pulses of microwaves or radio waves and times the echoes from objects such as raindrops, aircraft or cars. The delay gives the distance, and the Doppler shift gives the speed.
Radiation sterilization, food irradiation
Killing microbes with gamma rays, electron beams or X-rays. Medical supplies already sealed in their packaging receive about 25 kGy, and foods from under 1 kGy up to 30 kGy for dried spices. The radiation does not make the products radioactive.
Radio bands: VLF, LF, MF, HF, VHF, UHF
Engineering names for ranges of radio frequency, each ten times higher than the one before: very low (3–30 kHz), low, medium, high, very high and ultra high frequency (300 MHz–3 GHz).
Radio telescope
A large dish, or an array of them, that collects radio waves and microwaves from space. Radio astronomers use the lines of molecules such as CO and H2O to find and study the cold gas clouds where stars form.
Radioactive decay
The spontaneous change of an unstable nucleus into another, giving off radiation: an alpha particle, a beta particle or a positron, and often gamma rays. Each radioactive isotope decays at its own rate, set by its half-life.
Radiotherapy
Treating cancer with ionizing radiation, most often beams of high-energy X-rays (several MeV) from a linear accelerator, aimed so that they meet at the tumor.
Raman spectroscopy
Shining a laser on a sample and measuring the light it scatters. A tiny fraction comes back shifted in wavelength by the energies of the molecule’s vibrations. It complements infrared: vibrations that change how easily the electron cloud is distorted, such as the symmetric stretch of CO2, show up in Raman even when they do not absorb infrared.
Reduced mass, \(\mu\)
The effective mass of two bonded atoms vibrating or rotating about their center: \(\mu = \dfrac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\). It is always smaller than the lighter of the two masses.
Resonance
Strong absorption that happens only when the frequency of the radiation matches a natural frequency of the system, such as the precession frequency of a nucleus in NMR. There the resonance condition is \(\nu = \gamma B_0/2\pi\): only radio waves of this frequency flip the nuclear spin.
Resonant cavity
A hollow metal chamber in which waves of certain frequencies bounce back and forth in step and build up, much as a bottle hums at one note when you blow across it. The cavities of a magnetron set its frequency, and a microwave oven is itself a cavity.
Retinal and opsin
Retinal is the light-absorbing molecule of the eye, a short conjugated chain made from vitamin A; an opsin is the protein that holds it. When retinal absorbs a photon, one of its double bonds flips from cis to trans, which starts the signal sent to the brain.
Rotational constant, \(B\)
A constant for each molecule that sets the spacing of its rotational energy levels: \(B = h/(8\pi^2 c I)\), usually given in cm−1. Heavier atoms or a longer bond give a larger moment of inertia \(I\) and so a smaller \(B\). For CO, \(B = 1.9225\ \text{cm}^{-1}\).
Rotational quantum number, \(J\)
The whole number (0, 1, 2, 3, …) that labels the rotational energy levels of a molecule, \(E_J = hcB\,J(J+1)\). When a molecule absorbs a microwave photon it moves up exactly one level. Not to be confused with J, the symbol for joules.
Rydberg constant, \(R_{\text{H}}\)
The constant in the formula for hydrogen’s spectral lines, \(1/\lambda = R_{\text{H}}(1/n_f^2 - 1/n_i^2)\): \(R_{\text{H}} = 1.0968 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m}^{-1}\).
Scintillation and germanium detectors
Two kinds of gamma-ray detector. In a scintillator, such as a crystal of sodium iodide (NaI), each gamma ray makes a flash of light whose brightness measures its energy. In a germanium detector, cooled with liquid nitrogen to about −196 °C, the gamma ray frees electrons directly, which measures its energy about 25 times more sharply.
Selection rule
A rule for which jumps between energy levels can absorb or emit light. In infrared spectroscopy, a vibration absorbs only if it changes the molecule’s dipole moment; in rotational spectroscopy, the rotational quantum number \(J\) changes by exactly 1.
Series limit
The shortest wavelength (highest energy) line of a spectral series, given off when a free electron, from \(n = \infty\), drops straight to the series’ lower level. The lines of a series crowd closer and closer together toward it.
Shell, subshell
A shell is the set of orbitals with the same principal quantum number n (1, 2, 3, …). Each shell splits into subshells of slightly different energy: s (holds 2 electrons), p (6), d (10) and f (14).
Short-wavelength limit, \(\lambda_{\min}\)
The shortest X-ray wavelength an X-ray tube can make, given off when an electron turns all of its kinetic energy into a single photon: \(\lambda_{\min} = hc/(eV)\), where \(e\) is the charge of an electron and \(V\) the tube voltage. At 30 kV it is 0.041 nm.
Soft and hard X-rays
Soft X-rays have lower energies, roughly 0.1–10 keV, and are easily absorbed, even by air. Hard X-rays, above about 10 keV, get through much more and are used for medical images and airport scanners.
SPECT and the gamma camera
A gamma camera is a large scintillation crystal behind a lead plate pierced by many parallel holes, which records where gamma rays leave the body, usually from technetium-99m (140 keV). Turning it around the patient builds a 3D image: single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT).
Spectrometer, spectrophotometer
An instrument that spreads radiation out into its wavelengths (or frequencies) and measures how much there is of each. A spectrophotometer measures how much of each wavelength a sample absorbs, by comparing the light before and after the sample.
Spectroscopy
Measuring which wavelengths a sample absorbs or emits, and using the pattern to identify the substance, work out its structure, or find how much of it is present.
Speed of light, \(c\)
The speed of all electromagnetic radiation in a vacuum: \(c = 2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}\), or \(2.998 \times 10^{10}\ \text{cm s}^{-1}\). It links wavelength and frequency, \(c = \lambda\nu\).
SPF and broad spectrum
The sun protection factor, SPF, tells how many times longer skin protected by a sunscreen takes to redden than unprotected skin; it measures protection mostly against UVB. “Broad spectrum” on the label means the sunscreen also blocks UVA, judged by how far into the UVA its absorption reaches.
Spin–spin splitting (the n + 1 rule)
The splitting of an NMR signal into several lines by the spins of hydrogens on neighboring carbon atoms. A hydrogen with n equivalent neighbors gives n + 1 lines: in ethanol the CH3 signal, next to a CH2, is a triplet, and the CH2 signal is a quartet.
Standing wave, hot spot
A wave pattern that stays in one place, made when waves bouncing between walls overlap. In a microwave oven it leaves hot spots about 6 cm apart (half the 12.2 cm wavelength) with cooler spots between them; the turntable carries food through both.
State symbols
Letters after each formula in a chemical equation that give its physical state: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas and (aq) dissolved in water.
Stefan–Boltzmann law, \(\sigma\)
The power a hot object gives off from each square meter rises as the fourth power of its temperature: \(P = \sigma T^4\), where \(\sigma = 5.670 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\,\text{K}^{-4}\) and \(T\) is in kelvin.
Stimulated emission
Emission of a photon by an excited atom or molecule when another photon of the same energy passes by. The new photon is an exact copy of the first, traveling in step with it. It is the process that amplifies light in a laser.
Stokes shift
The difference in wavelength between the light a molecule absorbs and the longer-wavelength light it gives off by fluorescence. It arises because the excited molecule loses some energy as heat before it emits.
Stretching and bending vibrations
The two basic kinds of molecular vibration. In a stretch, a bond gets longer and shorter (symmetric when bonds stretch together, asymmetric when one stretches as another shortens); in a bend, the angle between bonds changes. Stretches usually take more energy, so they absorb at higher wavenumbers.
Superheated water
Water heated above its normal boiling point without boiling, which can happen in a smooth, clean container in a microwave. A small disturbance can then make it boil violently all at once.
Synchrotron
A ring-shaped accelerator in which electrons circling at nearly the speed of light give off intense, tunable light from the infrared to hard X-rays, used for spectroscopy and crystallography.
Tapetum lucidum
A mirror-like layer behind the retina of many night-active animals, such as foxes, cats and deer. It reflects light back through the retina for a second chance to be seen, which makes their eyes shine in a beam of light.
Tesla, T
The SI unit of magnetic field strength. For scale: the Earth’s field is about 0.00005 T, a fridge magnet about 0.005 T, an MRI scanner 1.5 to 3 T, and research NMR magnets about 7 to 28 T.
Thermal imaging (thermography)
Making a picture from the infrared that objects give off because of their temperature, usually at 8–14 µm. It works in complete darkness, because it does not need any light to be reflected.
Thymine dimer
Two neighboring thymine bases in a strand of DNA joined by new bonds after the DNA absorbs ultraviolet light. The kink blocks copying of the DNA until it is repaired; damage left unrepaired can lead to skin cancer, and in microbes it stops them from multiplying.
UV index
A number given in weather reports for how strongly the Sun’s ultraviolet can burn the skin, found by weighting the sunlight at each wavelength by how easily it causes sunburn. A value of 3 or more calls for sun protection, and 11 or more is extreme.
UVA, UVB, UVC and vacuum UV
The parts of the ultraviolet: UVA from 315 to 400 nm, UVB from 280 to 315 nm, UVC from 100 to 280 nm, and vacuum UV below about 200 nm, which air absorbs. The ozone layer absorbs all of the Sun’s UVC and much of its UVB.
Valence electrons
The electrons in an atom’s outermost shell, the ones involved in bonding. They are held most loosely, so visible or ultraviolet light can excite them, and ultraviolet photons can remove them.
Vasodilation
Widening of small blood vessels, which lets more blood flow through. Warming tissue, for example with infrared heat, causes it.
Vitamin D
A vitamin the body needs to absorb calcium for healthy bones. The skin makes it when UVB light turns 7-dehydrocholesterol into previtamin D3, which then changes into vitamin D3.
Waveguide
A hollow metal pipe, usually rectangular, that carries microwaves from one place to another by bouncing them between its walls, as from the magnetron into the cooking chamber of an oven.
Wavelength, \(\lambda\)
The distance from one crest of a wave to the next.
Wavenumber, \(\tilde{\nu}\)
The number of waves per centimeter, \(1/\lambda\), in cm−1. Infrared and Raman spectra use it because it is proportional to energy.
Wien’s law
The wavelength at which a hot object glows most brightly falls as it gets hotter: \(\lambda_{\max} = (2.898 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K})/T\), with \(T\) in kelvin.
Work function, \(\varphi\)
The smallest energy needed to pull an electron out through the surface of a solid. In X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy it is a small, known correction for the spectrometer.
X-ray crystallography
Finding where every atom in a crystal sits from the pattern of spots made when X-rays diffract from its planes of atoms (Bragg’s law).
X-ray fluorescence (XRF)
Identifying elements from the X-rays they give off after X-rays knock out their core electrons. Each element’s lines fall at its own energies, as Moseley’s law describes.
X-ray tube
A vacuum tube in which electrons from a hot filament (the cathode) are accelerated by tens of kilovolts into a metal target (the anode). As they slow down in the target they give off a continuous spectrum of X-rays (bremsstrahlung), plus the characteristic lines of the target metal.
